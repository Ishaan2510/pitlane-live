### The three worst races (leave-one-driver-out error per lap)

- 2024 round 8, Monaco Grand Prix: 3.58 s. Red flag on lap 1: everyone changed tyres during the stoppage and then ran to the end without stopping, so only 6 drivers have a second stint to test on, and at Monaco the pace is set by the car in front rather than by the tyres.
- 2024 round 9, Canadian Grand Prix: 2.51 s. Wet start on a drying track: slicks only went on for the last third, and the track gained time every lap, which the model reads as tyres getting quicker with age (negative deg rates).
- 2026 round 16, Bahrain Grand Prix: 1.70 s. A heavily interrupted race: about 320 driver laps under yellow, Safety Car or VSC and 73 pit stops, so the green runs are short, and one car ran intermediates in the opening laps.

## What the model gets right

On a normal dry race the fit explains most of the lap-to-lap variation (median R² 0.78) and a driver the model has never seen is predicted to a median 0.53 s per lap, using nothing but their first-stint pace and the tyres they were on. The ordering of the compounds, the size of the fuel effect and the difference between a low-wear and a high-wear track come out of the data without being told, and 94 of 167 compound fits meet the high-confidence rule.

## Where it fails

The error does not average out over a stint: the median stint is off by 6.5 s in total, which is the same order as the gaps a strategy call is fought over. The model's race is also quicker than the real one (median 0.6 s over the green racing laps), because it is fitted on clean laps only. 20 compound fits have a negative deg rate, 9 races needed the fuel prior, and 3 races are not modelled at all.

## Why

Three things the lap times cannot tell apart. Traffic: a car stuck behind another laps slower on any tyre, and the model reads part of that as wear. Driver management: drivers lift to make a stint last, so the measured degradation is the degradation of a managed tyre, and a tyre pushed to the limit would wear faster than the fit says. Mixed conditions: a drying or rubbering-in track makes every lap faster regardless of tyre age, which the single fuel slope only partly absorbs (that is where the negative deg rates come from). Fuel burn and tyre age also rise together within a stint, so the two are only separated by stops at different times; when a race does not give enough of that, the fuel slope is fixed at the prior and the deg rates inherit its error.
